Cost is an architectural constraint, not a finance problem

Architecture decisions set the cost curve years before anyone looks at the bill. Being able to express a design in cost per unit of business value is one of the clearest seniority signals there is.

Worked example: cost per 1,000 orders

Volume
1.2M orders/month, peak 40× on sale days
Compute
12 web + 6 worker pods, 2 vCPU / 4 GiB each
Database
primary + replica, provisioned for peak
Kafka
3 brokers, 7-day retention
Egress
the line everyone forgets until the invoice

Divide the monthly total by 1,200 and you have cost per 1,000 orders. Now every proposal has a comparable number: does this cache pay for itself? Does that extra service? Does multi-region?

It also exposes the real bill-driver, which in most enterprise systems is the database provisioned for peak and idle the rest of the month — not the application tier everyone spends time tuning.

Decisions with long cost tails

  • Provisioning for peak. A 40× sale peak sized statically means paying 40× all year. Autoscaling, queueing and load shedding are cost decisions as much as resilience ones.
  • Chatty synchronous calls. Each internal hop costs compute, network and latency budget on every request, forever.
  • Retention. Seven days of Kafka versus thirty is a straight multiple. So is log retention, which is frequently the largest observability line.
  • Cross-AZ and cross-region traffic. Priced per gigabyte, invisible in design reviews, and often the biggest surprise on a first multi-region bill.
  • One service per team, by policy. Each one carries a fixed floor of compute, monitoring and pipeline cost regardless of its traffic.
Capacity planning, minimallyCurrent peak throughput × expected growth × a safety factor, checked against the measured limit from a load test. Re-run quarterly. That is enough to avoid both the surprise outage and the 60%-idle cluster.
