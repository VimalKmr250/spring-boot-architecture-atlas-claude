Three questions that separate mid-level from senior answers

These are the follow-ups asked once the design works. A strong answer names the shared state, the invariant and the atomic step before naming any tool, and says what changes when the design leaves a single JVM.

Q10

Two users book the same seat at the same moment. What happens in your design?

30-second answerExactly one wins, because claiming a seat is a single atomic operation: putIfAbsent on the show's claim map, or an insert against a unique key in the database. A multi-seat hold claims seats in sorted order and releases everything on the first conflict, so there are no partial holds. Holds carry an expiry that is checked whenever someone wants the seat, so an abandoned hold frees itself.

Go deeper: Cover the payment race. Charge first, then confirm under the booking's own lock only if the hold is still alive; if it expired during payment, refund. "Confirm" and "expire" cannot both succeed, because both go through that lock. For many servers, the same three steps are an insert on a unique key, a conditional update that takes over a dead hold, and a delete that releases only your own claim.

Q11

Design a thread-safe LRU cache.

30-second answerA hash map from key to node for O(1) lookup, and a doubly linked list of nodes in recency order, with sentinel head and tail. get moves the node to the front; put adds at the front and evicts from the back when full. For thread safety, one lock around each operation, because get modifies the list. That also rules out a read-write lock.

Go deeper: Offer the scaling options: striped segments (approximate LRU, less contention), or a concurrent map with buffered recency updates, which is Caffeine's approach and what you would use in production. Mention the loading-cache follow-up: never load while holding the lock, and use a map of in-flight futures so a hundred concurrent misses trigger one load.

Q12

Your design now runs on ten servers. What changes?

30-second answerThe classes stay; their guardians move. In-memory maps become tables, and the database becomes the source of truth. Every check-then-act becomes a conditional write or a unique constraint, which enforces the invariant for all servers at once. Ids come from sequences or UUIDs, not an AtomicInteger. Retries need idempotency keys, and in-process events go through an outbox to a broker.

Go deeper: Name the shard key: the aggregate no transaction crosses, such as the lot, the show or the group. Name what may be stale (seat maps, free-spot displays) and what may not (claims, debits). Say you would avoid distributed locks by making writes conditional, and if you needed one, use a lease with a fencing token. Finally, name the hot spot a shard cannot fix, such as a blockbuster's opening night, and put a waiting room in front of it.
