Library management: a title is not a copy, and the rules depend on the member

The trap in this problem is modelling "book" as one class. A library owns several physical copies of one title: you borrow a copy, but you search for and reserve a title. Get that split right in the first five minutes, and loans, holds and the waiting list all fall into place.

Functional requirements

  1. The catalogue holds books (ISBN, title, authors), each with one or more copies identified by a barcode.
  2. Some copies are reference only: they can be read in the library but never borrowed.
  3. Members have a tier: standard members may hold 3 loans for 14 days, premium members 5 loans for 28 days.
  4. Returning late adds a fine of 10 per day, capped at 500. A member with unpaid fines cannot borrow.
  5. When no copy of a title is available, a member can reserve it and join a first-come, first-served waiting list.
  6. A returned copy with a waiting list goes on hold for the first member for 3 days, and that member is notified. No one else can borrow it meanwhile.
  7. Search by title or author, case-insensitive.

Questions to ask first

Branches?
One branch. Several is a follow-up.
Renewals?
Not now; the design should allow them.
Fine payment?
Recorded as paid in full; no payment gateway.
Reserve when a copy is on the shelf?
No: go and borrow it.
Hold expiry?
Stored on the copy; a daily job moves expired holds on. Out of scope for the code.
Notification channel?
Any; the library only raises the event.

Nouns, and what they become

LibraryCopyMember Book · recordLoan · recordTier · enumCopyStatus · enum FinePolicy · interfaceLibraryListener · interface

Not classes: librarian (a caller with more permissions), shelf, rack (a location field if asked). Reservation is a member id in a per-title queue until it needs data of its own.

Say the title/copy split out loud"A Book is the catalogue entry, one per ISBN. A Copy is a physical item with a barcode and a status. Loans point at copies; reservations point at books." It is the single sentence that most often separates a pass from a fail on this problem.
