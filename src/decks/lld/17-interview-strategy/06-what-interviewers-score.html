What interviewers score, and what each score looks like from their side of the table

Rubrics differ by company, but the dimensions are stable. Most interviewers write notes under five or six headings and decide from the pattern. Knowing the headings tells you where the minutes should go: a flawless diagram with no code scores well on one heading and badly on three.

DimensionWeak signalStrong signal
RequirementsStarts coding at once; or asks twenty questionsFive sharp questions, a written scope, stated assumptions
Object modellingA god class; anaemic entities; one class for title and copyBehaviour next to data; values as records; the right splits
Design principlesPatterns named with no reason; or a switch on type everywhereInterfaces exactly at the stated variation points, and a reason for each
Code qualityPublic fields, setters for everything, double for money, no validationEncapsulated state, validated boundaries, clear names, small methods
CorrectnessNever runs it; misses the double-redeem or the rounding caseDemo or test for the main flow and two edges, unprompted
ExtensibilityThe change request touches everythingNames the classes that change before editing; the change is local
Concurrency (mid/senior)synchronized on everything, or nothingNames shared state, the invariant and the atomic unit; picks the tool
CommunicationSilent typing, or defensive when challengedNarrates decisions, invites correction, handles pushback with trade-offs
By levelJunior: modelling and working code carry the decision. Mid-level: add clean principles and edge cases. Senior: all of that plus concurrency, failure handling and extension, raised by you rather than drawn out by questions. See Why companies interview for it.
The tie-breakerWhen two candidates both produce working designs, the one who pointed out a weakness in their own design, and said how they would fix it, almost always gets the stronger write-up.
