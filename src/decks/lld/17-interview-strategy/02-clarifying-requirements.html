Clarifying requirements: the questions that change the design, and none that do not

A good clarifying question has an answer that changes a class, a relationship or a lock. "What colour are the cars?" changes nothing. "Can a car park in a bigger spot?" changes the allocation rule. Ask five or six questions of the first kind, write down the answers, and state your own assumption for anything the interviewer leaves open.

Ask aboutExampleIt decides
Actors"Who uses it: customers, staff, other systems?"Which interfaces and permissions exist
Core use cases"The three things it must do, in priority order?"What gets coded first
Variation"Can the pricing rule change? Per lot, or per day?"Where the interfaces go
Cardinality"One lot or many? Several copies of a title?"Multiplicities, and the title/copy-style splits
Lifecycle"Does a hold expire? Can a booking be cancelled after payment?"States and transitions
Concurrency"Several entry gates? Many users on one show?"Locks and atomic operations
Failure"What if payment fails, or the dispenser jams?"Ordering and compensation
Scale, briefly"In memory for now, or should I assume a database?"Repositories, ids and time
Write it down like this, where both of you can see it
IN SCOPE
  1. park: assign smallest free spot that fits, issue ticket
  2. leave: fee = hourly rate by vehicle type, part hours round up
  3. show free spots per floor

OUT OF SCOPE
  payments, reservations, EV charging

ASSUMPTIONS
  - larger spot allowed if own size is full
  - several gates call park() concurrently
  - one lot, in memory
Two ways to get this phase wrongAsking nothing and designing the parking lot you once read about, which the interviewer then changes under you. Or asking twenty questions and reaching minute twelve with no classes. Five to six questions, then "I'll assume the rest; stop me if any of that is wrong."
