Delegate login to an identity provider; services verify tokens locally

Authentication answers who you are, and authorization answers what you may do. In a modern design, neither lives in each service. An identity provider runs login and MFA and issues signed tokens through OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect. Every API verifies the token's signature with a cached public key, so no service stores a password and none calls the identity provider on every request.

Sessions or JWTs

A server-side session (an opaque id plus Redis) can be revoked instantly and is small, but every request needs a lookup. A JWT needs no lookup, but it can't be revoked before it expires. So keep access tokens short-lived (5–15 min), rotate refresh tokens, and keep a deny-list for emergencies. For browsers, a BFF keeping tokens server-side behind an HttpOnly cookie is the safest pattern.

Authorization models

RBAC: roles grant permissions ("admin"). ABAC: rules over attributes ("owner, or same tenant and during business hours"). ReBAC: relationships, as in Google Zanzibar or OpenFGA ("is an editor of this doc's folder"). Coarse scopes are checked at the gateway. Object-level checks belong in the service, and skipping them is the top API vulnerability, BOLA.

Machines and partners

Service-to-service calls use mTLS identities (a mesh with SPIFFE ids) or the OAuth client-credentials flow. Federated identity (SSO over SAML or OIDC) lets enterprise customers log in with their own directory. The IdP brokers it, and your services see the same JWTs either way.
