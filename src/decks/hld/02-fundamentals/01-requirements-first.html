Functional requirements say what it does; non-functional ones decide the architecture

Two systems with identical features can need completely different designs. A URL shortener for an internal team fits on one box. The same shortener for a social network needs a CDN, a cache tier and a key-generation service. The non-functional requirements are what separate them, which is why step 1 exists.

If the requirement says……the architecture gets
Reads ≫ writes (100:1), p99 read < 50 msA cache in front of the store, read replicas, CDN for anything static, denormalised read models
99.99% availableNo single points of failure, at least two availability zones, automated fail-over, graceful degradation of non-core features
Strong consistency (money, inventory, bookings)A single leader per record, transactions or compare-and-set, idempotency keys. Accept the latency cost.
Traffic spikes 10× (sales, launches)Queues to absorb bursts, autoscaling on the stateless tier, load shedding and rate limits at the edge
Data must never be lostSynchronous replication or quorum writes, write-ahead logs, backups with tested restores, RPO ≈ 0
Global users, < 100 ms everywhereA CDN, geo-routing, regional read replicas, and possibly multi-region writes with conflict handling
GDPR / data residencyPer-region data stores, a deletion pipeline that reaches caches, logs and backups, encryption and audit trails
Questions worth asking every timeWho are the users, and how many are active each day? What is the read:write ratio? What must be fast, and what may be slow? What is the worst thing that can go wrong: losing data, showing stale data, or being down? Is it one region or global? What is explicitly out of scope?
