Design the system before you design the classes

High-level design (HLD) is where you decide what the components are, how data moves between them, where it is stored, and what happens when a piece fails or the load grows tenfold. This deck teaches that in three stages: the fundamentals and building blocks, the architectural and cloud patterns built from them, and then twelve interview designs worked step by step. It loosely follows the roadmap.sh system design path, and almost every idea comes with a diagram.

11

12

How to use it

  • Arrow keys or the buttons move through slides, and C opens the contents. Each worked design is its own section there.
  • Self-study: read one section per sitting, then redraw its diagrams from memory. If you can't redraw a diagram, you can't use it in an interview.
  • Teaching: use sections 02–11 as lectures. Use the designs as exercises: show the requirements slide, let the group sketch a design for 15 minutes, then reveal the rest.

What it assumes

  • You have built and deployed a web service with a database behind it. No distributed-systems background is needed.
  • Nothing is tied to one vendor, but real technology is named (PostgreSQL, Redis, Kafka, Cassandra, S3, a CDN), because interviewers want to hear concrete choices.
  • All numbers are rough, and each states its assumptions. In an interview, the reasoning behind a number counts for more than its precision.
The one habit worth buildingName the trade-off every time you choose something. "I'll use a cache" earns nothing. "I'll use a cache-aside Redis in front of the database. Profiles can be up to 60 seconds stale, and in return the database sees about 5% of reads" is the sentence that gets scored.
