Caches sit at every layer, and the earliest hit is the cheapest

A cache keeps a copy of expensive-to-get data somewhere faster. Between the user and the database there are half a dozen places to keep that copy, each closer, faster and less consistent than the one behind it. A good design caches each kind of data at the earliest layer where its freshness requirements still hold.

What is worth caching

Data that is read far more often than it's written, is expensive to compute or fetch, and can tolerate some staleness. Classic examples: product pages, user profiles, rendered feeds, permission sets, and the results of heavy aggregations.

What isn't

Data that must be exact at the moment you read it: balances at payment time, stock at checkout, a password hash during login. Also anything with a unique key per request (a near-zero hit ratio), and data cheaper to recompute than to fetch over the network.

In-process vs distributed

In-process is the fastest, but each instance holds its own copy, so N servers keep N inconsistent copies and N times the memory. Use it for tiny, slow-changing data or a 1–5 s shield in front of hot keys. Use a distributed cache for everything that must be shared or larger than one heap.
