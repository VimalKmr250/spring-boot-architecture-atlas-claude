Two indices that never go backwards

A sliding window answers questions about contiguous runs in one pass. The whole pattern rests on Fact 2 from two slides back: once the window [l, r] is invalid, stretching it further right never makes it valid again — so l never has a reason to move back. That one fact is what turns O(n²) into O(n).

The invariant

At the bottom of every iteration, the window [l, r] is valid. The loop body has exactly three moves, always in this order:

  1. Admit a[r] into the window state — the window may now be invalid.
  2. Restore validity by evicting from the left, one element at a time.
  3. Record the answer, now that the invariant holds again.

Getting step 3 before step 2 is the single most common bug in the pattern: you measure a window that was never legal.

Why it is O(n), not O(n²)

The inner while loop looks like it makes the whole thing quadratic. It does not, and the argument is worth being able to say out loud:

l only ever increases, and it is bounded by n. So across the entire run, the inner loop executes at most n times in total — not n times per iteration. Each index enters the window once through r and leaves it at most once through l: 2n operations, amortised O(1) per step.

Being able to say that out loud is what turns "I think it is linear" into an answer an interviewer accepts.

Applies when

The answer is a contiguous subarray or substring, and validity is monotone — once a window is invalid, extending it right cannot make it valid again.

Does not apply when

Values can be negative and you are summing — extending the window may help or hurt, so no valid shrink rule exists. Also when the chosen elements need not be adjacent, since a window can only ever describe a contiguous run.

Complexity

O(n) time, since each index is admitted once and evicted at most once. O(k) space for the window state, where k is the alphabet or distinct-value count — O(1) for a fixed int[128].
