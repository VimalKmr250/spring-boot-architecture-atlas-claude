Always expand the closest unfinished node

When every edge costs the same, a queue suffices. When edges carry different weights, a queue is no longer in distance order — so replace it with a priority queue. That one substitution is Dijkstra's algorithm, and it is the whole of this pattern.

The invariant

When a node is popped from the priority queue, its recorded distance is final. Nothing still in the queue can offer a shorter route, because every remaining path starts from something at least as far away.

That argument requires non-negative weights. With a negative edge, a longer-looking route could later become shorter, and the invariant collapses — which is why Dijkstra is simply wrong on such graphs rather than slow.

So: pop, finalise, relax the neighbours, repeat.

Lazy deletion

Java's PriorityQueue cannot decrease a key. The standard workaround is to push a new entry whenever a shorter distance is found, leaving the stale one in place.

Then skip stale entries on pop: if the popped distance exceeds the recorded one, discard it and continue. The queue may hold up to E entries, which is where the O(E log E) bound comes from.

Without that skip the algorithm still terminates and still returns the right answer — but it re-expands finalised nodes and wastes a great deal of time.

Applies when

Edges carry non-negative weights and you want the cheapest route. The cost need not be a distance — it can be time, effort, or a probability treated multiplicatively.

Does not apply when

Weights can be negative — use Bellman-Ford. Or when a constraint such as "at most k stops" means a cheaper partial route is not always better; then the state must carry that constraint.

Complexity

O(E log E) with a binary heap and lazy deletion. Bellman-Ford is O(V·E), and the k-bounded variant O(k·E). Floyd–Warshall is O(V³), which is fine when V ≤ 100.
