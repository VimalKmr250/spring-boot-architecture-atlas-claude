Buy O(1) lookup with O(n) memory

The simplest trade in the deck and the one that removes the most nested loops. Any time the inner loop is asking "have I seen this?", "how many of these?" or "which of these belong together?", that loop is a hash lookup and the O(n²) collapses to O(n).

The three questions a hash answers

Membership
HashSet. "Have I seen x?" — LC 217 and LC 128.
Counting
HashMap<T, Integer> or int[]. "How many x?" — LC 242 and 347.
Grouping
HashMap<Key, List<T>>. "Which items share a property?" — LC 49. The craft is entirely in choosing the key.

A fourth use is the complement: instead of searching for a pair, store what you have seen and ask whether the partner you need has already gone by. That is LC 1, and it is one pass rather than two loops.

The canonical key

Grouping problems reduce to: find a function key(x) that is identical for items that belong together and different otherwise. Once you have it, the grouping is four lines.

For anagrams, two keys work. Sorting each string is O(L log L) and obvious. A 26-slot count vector is O(L) and strictly better — and generalises to alphabets where sorting is meaningless.

Say both in an interview. Reaching for the count vector unprompted signals that you think about the key rather than just calling sort.

Applies when

Order does not matter to the answer, and the inner loop is a lookup, a count or a grouping. Also when you need original indices, which sorting would destroy.

Does not apply when

You need order, rank or a range query — that is a sorted structure or a TreeMap. Also when O(1) extra space is required: the map is the O(n).

Complexity

O(n) time on average, O(n) space. Java's HashMap degrades to O(log n) per operation under collisions, not O(n), since Java 8 — buckets become red-black trees past eight entries.

Recognition cues — the literal words in the statement"return the indices" (order matters, so no sorting) · "group the anagrams" · "contains a duplicate" · "the k most frequent" · "the longest consecutive sequence" · "you may return the answer in any order" · "O(n) runtime" stated on a problem that looks quadratic. That last one is the strongest signal in this deck: a demanded O(n) on a pairing problem almost always means a hash map.
