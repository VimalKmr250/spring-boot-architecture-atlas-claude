Laziness and order: the questions behind the obvious ones

These three separate people who use streams from people who understand them. Each answer is easier to give after pressing through the stepper on slide 5 once.

Q4

What does it mean that streams are lazy? Why didn't my peek print anything?

30-second answerIntermediate operations do not run when you call them. They run when a terminal operation pulls elements, and then one element at a time: each element goes through every stage before the next is read. So unneeded work is never done — findFirst after filter stops at the first match — and a pipeline with no terminal operation does nothing at all, which is why a lone peek prints nothing.

Go deeper: Give the demonstration: filter, map and findFirst over fig, kiwi, apple, plum, cherry print "filter fig, filter kiwi, map kiwi" and stop. Then the Java 9 subtlety: count() on a source whose size is known (a List, even after map) returns the size without traversing, so a peek before it never runs. Laziness is also why streams can be infinite, and why sorted() is expensive: it is the point where the flow has to stop and wait.

Q5

What is the difference between stateless and stateful operations?

30-second answerA stateless operation handles each element on its own: filter, map, flatMap, peek. A stateful one must remember what it has seen: distinct keeps a set, sorted buffers everything, limit and skip keep a count. Stateful operations cost memory, cannot emit until they have enough input, and are the expensive ones in parallel.

Go deeper: Name the consequences. sorted() on an infinite stream never emits and ends in OutOfMemoryError. distinct() over millions of rows holds every distinct row. limit on an ordered parallel stream has to track positions. Then separate stateful operations from stateful lambdas, such as filter(seen::add) over a HashSet. The first is a cost the library manages. The second is your bug, and it breaks in parallel.

Q6

findFirst or findAny? forEach or forEachOrdered?

30-second answerfindFirst returns the first element in encounter order; findAny returns any element, which in parallel is whichever chunk answers first, and that is cheaper because it never waits for earlier chunks. Likewise, in parallel forEach processes elements in any order, while forEachOrdered keeps encounter order at the cost of parallelism. Sequentially, each pair behaves the same.

Go deeper: Encounter order comes from the source: a List has one, a HashSet does not, so over a HashSet findFirst has no "first" to honour. On JDK 25, a parallel findAny over the numbers 1 to 8 most often returned 6, and a parallel forEach printed 65378421. Use findAny for "is there one?" and findFirst when position matters. Both return an Optional, and both throw NullPointerException if the element they find is null.
